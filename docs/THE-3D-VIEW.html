The perspective view

It is the best thing in the cartridge, and the first thing to say is what it is not: the view of the course is never computed. There is no projection, no trigonometry, not one call into the tables the game uses for ball physics. The perspective comes pre-drawn in the ROM, hole by hole and stretch by stretch, and all the Z80 does is resolve two indirections and copy 8×8 patterns.

What follows is checked against the emulator's RAM and VRAM, not inferred: tools/omsx_vista.tcl freezes the machine at the exact instant a row is being painted and dumps both buffers.

The eighty maps

The table at 0x6A2B holds ninety-eight pointers in eighteen groups, one per hole; tabla_tramos_por_hoyo (0x5E54) says how many fall in each group, between four and six. The first of each group is 105 bytes and the rest are 294.

Those numbers tell the whole story: 105 is 21×5 and 294 is 21×14. The view is twenty-one columns wide, and it is assembled from two pieces:

piecesizewhat it is
first of the group21×5sky and horizon, the same for the whole hole
one per stretch21×14the ground, different in each stretch

0x5B17 decompresses the first into 0xEA00 and 0x5B27 the second into 0xEA69, which is exactly 0xEA00+105. And 0xEA69+294 lands on 0xEB8F, which is precisely where 0x56B7 leaves the scroll pointer. The two pieces end up butted together: 399 consecutive bytes, nineteen rows of twenty-one columns.

Once repeats are removed, the eighteen holes spend eighty ground maps. That is the entire course.

Three hops to paint one cell

None of those 399 bytes is a picture. Each one is an index, and it takes three hops to reach a pixel:

  1. The index goes into a list of sixteen-bit tile codes living at 0xE800. 0x5B61 walks it with add hl,bc twice, which is two-byte indexing.
  2. That list is not stored as such: 0xA8CB builds it when the hole starts, by decompressing two blocks and taking the running sum of their bytes. What the cartridge stores are differences.
  3. The code that comes out indexes tabla_patrones_terreno (0x8B5E), 570 uncompressed 8×8 patterns — and the colour arrives by a different road entirely: the chain of blocks at 0x9D2E, which 0x5EA5 walks header by header.

The 0xE800 list is built in two consecutive passes: first the codes shared by every hole — 129 or 154, depending which — and the hole's own behind them. That they sit flush is not a guess: the destination addresses the code writes by hand, 0xE902 and 0xE934, are exactly 0xE800 plus the size of the first one. Against the emulator's RAM both come out without a single differing byte.

Getting closer is a name-table scroll

There is no zoom. What sells the forward motion is that 0x5D3C paints one fresh row of twenty-one cells at a time, while the pointer at 0xE0AE steps through the map twenty-one at a time: backwards as you approach (0x5C3A adds −21 to it) and forwards as you retreat. The rest of the screen shifts by copying name-table strips from one part of VRAM to another.

And the ball picks the stretch: (0xE0B5) — the high byte of (0xE0B4) — is which zone of the hole you are in, and it is what chooses which of the four to six ground maps applies. That is why a hole is seen in chunks rather than all at once.

The golfer does not exist

There is no drawing of a golfer anywhere in the cartridge. What there is are pieces, and every pose is composed on the spot from up to nine overlapping 16×16 sprites, each in its own colour — the only way to get a multi-coloured figure out of an MSX1.

0x4F8A is the loop that places them. It reads a list of piece numbers and, for each one, goes to a three-byte table: how far up, how far left, and what colour. The pattern number is stored nowhere — it falls out of a calculation, index×4 + 0x60, at 0x4FBA — and a zero closes the list early.

That this reading is the right one shows up in the subtraction: across the eight pieces of the rest pose, (0xE099) minus the first byte gives 144 in all eight, and (0xE098) minus the second gives 80 in all eight. And the third byte matches the attribute's colour piece for piece.

And there are three golfers, not one

0x4BF8 looks at the club in (0xE114) and picks one of three complete sets:

All three have six swing frames, and in play they do not come in order: recorded in openMSX (tools/guiones/swing.tcl, just the space bar), the frame (0xE06E) changes every 8 frames: 1 while aiming; on the first press, 2 and, if the power has gone past 0x40 (mira_el_angulo_alto, 0x4DBB), 3, right at the top, which stays while the other bar rises; on the second, 2 again and then 4, 5 and 6. With little power, 3 never shows. Every recorded frame matches the pieces the ROM gives for its swing frame (tools/swing_cotejo.py); the GIFs are on the front page. And the split needs no guessing: the three position tables measure 105, 87 and 66 bytes, which at three bytes per piece is 35, 29 and 22 — exactly what each sprite block carries — and the three lists measure 54, 48 and 36, which is six frames of nine, eight and six.

The golfer grows with the club because the camera grows: with a short iron you stand over the ball, with a wood you stand back. It is the same idea as the view, solved the same way: instead of scaling anything, there are three drawings.

What it took to see it

The published listing called the eighty maps "golfer frames" and the three piece blocks "hole plans 15, 16 and 17". They were the wrong way round: what looked like the golfer was the course, and the three blocks nobody could place turned out to be the golfer.

Two numbers gave it away. That 0xEA69 minus 0xEA00 came to 105, and that 105 and 294 are both multiples of 21, suggested the width was 21 and not a sprite; and that the pattern came out of index×4 + 0x60 explained in one go the patterns 96, 100, 104… that the emulator's attribute table had loaded.
